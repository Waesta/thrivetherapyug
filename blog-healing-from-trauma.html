Trauma & Grief

Healing From Trauma: What to Expect in Trauma Counselling in Uganda

Trauma is far more common in Uganda than we publicly acknowledge — and far more treatable than many people believe. If you have been carrying something heavy from the past, this article will help you understand what trauma is, what it does to the brain and body, and what professional healing actually looks like in a Kampala counselling room.

Ahebwa Keisha Patience January 2025 12 min read Trauma & Grief

In Uganda, we are a resilient people. We carry our pain quietly, keep moving, and tell ourselves — and each other — to "be strong." And strength is real. But resilience was never meant to mean the permanent suppression of what has hurt us.

Whether it is the sudden death of a parent, surviving gender-based violence, living through a road accident on one of Uganda's highways, displacement, abuse during childhood, or the slow erosion of years spent in a harmful environment — trauma leaves marks. It changes the nervous system. It reshapes how we see the world, other people, and ourselves.

The most important thing we want you to know is this: trauma is not a life sentence. With the right support, healing is not only possible — it is the expected outcome. Trauma counselling at Thrive Therapy & Wellness in Kampala is built entirely around this conviction.

What Is Trauma?

Trauma is the lasting emotional and physiological response to an experience — or a series of experiences — that overwhelmed a person's ability to cope at the time. The key word is overwhelmed. Trauma is not about weakness. It is about the nervous system encountering more than it could process in the moment, and then getting stuck in that state.

Clinicians generally distinguish between several types:

Acute Trauma

This results from a single, identifiable event: a road accident, a robbery, a sexual assault, receiving news of a sudden death, a medical emergency. The event is discrete and time-limited, even if its effects are not.

Chronic Trauma

This results from repeated, prolonged exposure to distressing events: ongoing domestic violence, sustained emotional or physical abuse, chronic illness, persistent poverty and food insecurity, or living in an environment of constant threat. In Uganda, chronic trauma often develops in contexts that are normalised — which makes it harder to name and therefore harder to address.

Complex Trauma

Complex trauma typically begins early in life and involves repeated interpersonal harm — often within relationships that should have been safe. Childhood neglect, emotional abuse, growing up with a parent struggling with addiction, or early sexual abuse all fall into this category. Complex trauma tends to have the most wide-ranging effects on identity, relationships, and self-perception.

In Uganda, common trauma sources include: road traffic accidents (one of the leading causes of injury and death), gender-based violence, sudden bereavement, the legacy of civil conflict in northern Uganda, medical trauma from difficult childbirth or illness, and the chronic trauma of poverty and insecurity. None of these experiences make a person broken. But all of them deserve professional attention.

How Trauma Affects Your Mind and Body

One of the most important shifts in trauma understanding over the last 30 years is the recognition that trauma is not just a psychological problem — it is a whole-body experience.

When a person encounters a threatening experience, the brain's alarm system — the amygdala — fires immediately. It floods the body with stress hormones (adrenaline, cortisol) and prepares it for fight, flight, or freeze. This is survival biology at its finest.

In most cases, after the threat has passed, the nervous system returns to baseline. But when a trauma is overwhelming, or when the environment never truly becomes safe, this stress response does not switch off. The nervous system remains on high alert — scanning constantly for danger, reacting to cues that remind it of the original event, and interpreting neutral situations as threatening.

This shows up in the body as:

  • Chronic muscle tension, particularly in the neck, shoulders, jaw, and hips
  • Digestive problems — nausea, IBS, appetite loss or emotional eating
  • Headaches and migraines
  • Fatigue that does not resolve with rest
  • A weakened immune system and frequent illness
  • Heart palpitations and shortness of breath
  • Chronic pain with no clear medical explanation

In Uganda, many people with unresolved trauma spend years visiting clinics for physical complaints — and leave without ever being asked about what they have been through. The body keeps the record long after the mind tries to move on.

Signs You May Be Carrying Unhealed Trauma

Trauma does not always announce itself clearly. Sometimes it appears as personality traits, habits, or relationship patterns that people do not connect to past experiences. Here are some of the most common signs:

  • Intrusive memories or flashbacks — unwanted, vivid re-experiencing of the event, sometimes triggered by sounds, smells, or situations
  • Nightmares — disturbing dreams related to the traumatic event, or simply persistent, disrupted sleep
  • Hypervigilance — being constantly "on guard," startling easily, scanning exits in rooms, unable to relax even in objectively safe situations
  • Emotional numbness — feeling disconnected from emotions, other people, or your own life; going through the motions without feeling present
  • Avoidance — steering clear of people, places, topics, or activities that remind you of the traumatic event
  • Intense, disproportionate anger — reacting to small frustrations with rage that surprises even you
  • Difficulty trusting people — expecting betrayal, pushing people away, or becoming dependent in ways that feel out of your control
  • Shame and self-blame — persistently feeling that what happened was your fault, or that you are fundamentally damaged or unworthy
  • Relationship difficulties — patterns of conflict, emotional unavailability, or attraction to harmful relationship dynamics
  • Using substances or behaviours to cope — alcohol, food, overworking, or excessive phone use to avoid or numb internal pain

If you recognise several of these in yourself, please do not use this as a reason to judge yourself. These are adaptations — the nervous system and psyche doing their best to keep you functioning after something overwhelming. They made sense at some point. Counselling helps you develop new responses that serve your current life.

What Happens in Your First Trauma Counselling Session

For many people in Uganda, the idea of sitting across from a counsellor and talking about painful experiences is frightening. A common fear is: "If I open this door, I will fall apart." It is worth addressing this directly — because good trauma therapy does not work by tearing the door off its hinges.

Your first session at Thrive Therapy & Wellness is primarily about safety and assessment. Here is what actually happens:

  1. Welcome and orientation — Patience introduces herself, explains how sessions work, discusses confidentiality, and answers any questions you have before anything else is discussed
  2. Your story, at your pace — you are invited to share what has brought you in, in whatever way feels comfortable. You are never pushed to disclose more than you are ready to share
  3. Assessment — Patience gently explores your current symptoms, how long you have been experiencing them, how they are affecting your daily life, and any relevant history
  4. Stabilisation first — before any deep trauma processing begins, the foundation of safe therapy is establishing coping tools: grounding techniques, breathing exercises, and a stable therapeutic relationship
  5. Collaborative planning — together, you discuss what your goals are and what a therapeutic process might look like for you

You will not be asked to relive your worst memories in the first session. Trauma therapy done well is a gradual, paced process that always keeps your wellbeing at the centre.

Evidence-Based Trauma Therapies Used at Thrive

Thrive Therapy & Wellness uses therapeutic approaches that have strong research support for trauma treatment. These are adapted to the cultural and social contexts of Ugandan clients — because generic Western therapy frameworks do not always translate meaningfully to Ugandan lived experience.

Cognitive Behavioural Therapy (CBT) for Trauma

Trauma-focused CBT helps clients identify and gently challenge the thought patterns that trauma has created — such as "I am not safe anywhere," "I deserved what happened," or "I cannot trust anyone." By examining these beliefs and testing them against present-day reality, clients gradually shift out of a trauma worldview and into one that allows for safety, connection, and hope. CBT also includes practical tools for managing flashbacks, anxiety, and avoidance behaviours.

Narrative Therapy

Narrative therapy is particularly well-suited to Ugandan contexts because it works through the power of story — something deeply embedded in Ugandan culture. In narrative therapy, the traumatic event is externalised — it is something that happened to you, not something that defines you. Clients are helped to retell their story in ways that reclaim agency, identify personal strengths, and create a different understanding of who they are beyond what they have suffered.

Somatic (Body-Based) Approaches

Because trauma lives in the body, healing often requires body-based interventions alongside talking therapy. At Thrive, this includes somatic awareness exercises — learning to notice and gently work with physical sensations associated with trauma — as well as grounding techniques that help regulate the nervous system in the moment. These approaches are accessible, practical, and do not require any prior experience with meditation or mindfulness.

Grief Work

Trauma and grief are deeply intertwined. Many Ugandans carry unprocessed grief — for people lost, for safety lost, for a version of themselves that was changed by what happened. Thrive's approach to trauma and grief counselling honours the full dimension of loss, including the spiritual and communal aspects that are central to Ugandan culture.

How Long Does Trauma Healing Take?

This is the question almost every client asks in their first session — and it deserves an honest answer rather than a comfortable one.

Healing from trauma is not linear. It does not follow a neat schedule. Some people notice significant improvement within eight to twelve sessions. Others, particularly those with complex or early-life trauma, benefit from a longer-term therapeutic relationship spanning many months. A small number of sessions — even three or four — can produce meaningful shifts in understanding and symptom management, even if full processing takes longer.

Several factors influence the pace of healing:

  • The type, severity, and duration of the original trauma
  • Whether the trauma occurred in childhood or adulthood
  • The presence of a stable, supportive environment outside of therapy
  • Whether the person is currently safe, or whether they remain in a harmful situation
  • Overall physical health and sleep quality
  • Access to consistent, ongoing sessions

What we can say with confidence is this: people who engage in trauma therapy consistently improve. The brain is neuroplastic — it can form new pathways, create new responses, and release old patterns. Healing is not about forgetting. It is about integrating what happened so that it no longer controls your present.

Is It Safe to Talk About My Trauma?

This concern is valid and important. There are forms of trauma work that are not safe — that push people to re-experience their worst moments without adequate support, leaving them flooded and worse off than before. This is not what happens at Thrive.

Confidentiality: Everything you share in a session at Thrive Therapy & Wellness is strictly confidential. Patience operates under professional ethical guidelines that protect your privacy absolutely, with the only exceptions being situations involving immediate risk to your safety or the safety of others — and even then, the approach is collaborative.

Pacing: You set the pace. Good trauma therapy is never about confronting as much as possible as quickly as possible. The therapeutic relationship must first be strong enough to hold the work. Stabilisation always precedes processing. If at any point a session feels too intense, you have the absolute right to slow down, redirect, or stop.

No pressure to disclose everything: Some clients take weeks or months before they feel ready to speak directly about the traumatic event — and that is completely acceptable. Healing can happen even while the details remain protected. What matters is the therapeutic process, not the full disclosure of every detail.

"It takes extraordinary courage to walk into a counsellor's room and say 'something happened to me and I need help.' In my work, I am always aware of that courage. My role is not to push, expose, or fix — it is to walk beside someone as they find their own way back to themselves. And they always do." — Ahebwa Keisha Patience, Counselling Psychologist & Founder, Thrive Therapy & Wellness
