Childhood Trauma in Adults: Recognising the Signs in Your Life

Many adults in Uganda are living with the effects of childhood trauma without ever connecting those experiences to the patterns, struggles, and pain they experience today. This is how to recognise what happened — and begin to heal.

Ahebwa Keisha Patience November 2024 10 min read Trauma

Many of the adults who come to Thrive Therapy & Wellness in Ntinda, Kampala do not initially identify childhood trauma as their presenting concern. They come because of anxiety they cannot explain. Relationships that keep failing in the same ways. A persistent sense of not being good enough, no matter what they achieve. Anger that arrives too fast and too hard. An inability to trust, even people who have given them no reason to distrust.

As therapy progresses, patterns emerge — and often, their roots trace back to experiences in childhood that were never processed, acknowledged, or healed. Childhood is when we form our most fundamental beliefs about ourselves, other people, and the world. When those years contain significant pain, fear, loss, or abuse — and most of us in Uganda received little to no professional support for those experiences — those beliefs and survival patterns travel with us into adulthood.

This article is for adults who suspect that something from their past might be shaping their present — but are not sure how to name it, or what to do about it.

What Is Childhood Trauma?

Childhood trauma is not limited to the most dramatic or obviously harmful experiences. While physical abuse, sexual abuse, and witnessing violence are clearly traumatic, childhood trauma also includes:

  • Emotional abuse: Being consistently criticised, humiliated, shamed, or told you are worthless or unwanted
  • Emotional neglect: Having emotional needs chronically unmet — not being comforted, listened to, or made to feel valued
  • Physical neglect: Chronic lack of food, clothing, healthcare, or physical safety
  • Loss: Death of a parent or caregiver, especially if the grief was unacknowledged or suppressed
  • Parental separation: Divorce, abandonment, or a parent who was physically present but emotionally unavailable
  • Growing up with a parent who had addiction, mental illness, or untreated trauma
  • Domestic violence in the home: Even if the child was not the direct target
  • Bullying or social exclusion: Severe or chronic peer rejection, particularly in early school years
  • Community violence: Particularly relevant for those who grew up in areas affected by the LRA conflict, civil unrest, or intercommunal violence in Uganda

Researchers use the term Adverse Childhood Experiences (ACEs) to refer to this range of potentially traumatic events. Studies consistently show that the higher a person's ACE score — the number of categories of adverse childhood experience they were exposed to — the greater their risk of mental and physical health challenges in adulthood.

Why Childhood Trauma Persists Into Adulthood

The child's nervous system is not equipped to process overwhelming experiences the way a regulated adult's might be. When a child experiences something frightening, painful, or deeply distressing, and there is no safe adult available to help them regulate — to name what happened, validate their feelings, and help them feel safe again — the experience is stored in an unprocessed, emotionally raw form in the nervous system.

This is not a conscious choice or a personal failure. It is simply what the brain and body do when they cannot complete the processing cycle — they hold the experience, encoded as a threat, ready to be activated when anything in the present environment resembles what happened in the past.

This is why, decades later, a certain tone of voice, a kind of look, a specific situation — can trigger a reaction that feels completely disproportionate to the present moment. Because in some part of the nervous system, the present moment is not the present moment at all. It is 1995, and you are nine years old, and the threat is real.

Signs of Unhealed Childhood Trauma in Adults

Unprocessed childhood trauma manifests differently in different people, but some of the most common patterns include:

In Relationships

  • Intense fear of abandonment — clinging to relationships even when they are unhealthy
  • Difficulty trusting partners, even those who have earned trust
  • Swinging between craving closeness and feeling overwhelmed by it
  • Choosing partners who recreate familiar patterns of childhood pain
  • Difficulty with vulnerability — keeping emotional walls firmly in place
  • Becoming the caretaker in every relationship, unable to receive care in return

In Emotional Regulation

  • Explosive or disproportionate anger triggered by seemingly small things
  • Emotional numbness or disconnection — not feeling much at all
  • Anxiety that has no clear present-moment cause
  • Rapid, overwhelming emotional flooding that feels impossible to control
  • Persistent shame — a background feeling of being fundamentally flawed or defective

In Sense of Self

  • Chronic low self-esteem that achievements do not seem to shift
  • People-pleasing — difficulty saying no, prioritising others' needs over your own survival
  • Perfectionism driven by a deep fear of failure or rejection
  • Feeling like an impostor — that you will be "found out" as inadequate
  • Difficulty identifying or articulating your own feelings, desires, or needs

In Body and Behaviour

  • Chronic physical symptoms with no clear medical cause — persistent pain, digestive issues, fatigue
  • Hypervigilance — always scanning for danger, startling easily, never fully relaxing
  • Dissociation — zoning out, feeling detached from your body or surroundings
  • Sleep difficulties — nightmares, insomnia, or sleeping excessively as a form of escape
  • Using substances, food, sex, work, or other behaviours to manage or numb painful emotions

Trauma in the Ugandan Context

Uganda has a complex history that makes childhood trauma particularly common and particularly underacknowledged. The LRA conflict displaced millions and exposed children to extreme violence. The AIDS epidemic of the 1980s and 1990s left an entire generation of children orphaned or growing up with gravely ill parents. Poverty, food insecurity, and overcrowded living conditions were — and remain — common. Corporal punishment in homes and schools was normalised for generations and is still common in many areas.

This does not mean that everyone who grew up in these circumstances is severely traumatised — human resilience is remarkable. But it does mean that childhood trauma in Uganda is widespread, frequently unnamed, and rarely given the professional support it deserves. The result is a generation of adults carrying wounds that affect their relationships, their parenting, their work, and their sense of self — often without any understanding of why.

What Trauma-Informed Therapy Can Do

The good news is that childhood trauma is not a life sentence. The brain retains neuroplasticity — the capacity to form new neural pathways — throughout life. With the right therapeutic support, it is possible to process old wounds, develop new patterns of relating to yourself and others, and build a life that is not defined by what happened to you as a child.

At Thrive Therapy & Wellness, our trauma-informed approach means that every element of the therapeutic relationship is designed with your nervous system's safety in mind. We do not push clients to revisit painful memories before they are ready. We work carefully and at a pace that feels manageable — building psychological resources and safety first, and moving into deeper processing only when the foundation is strong enough.

Approaches we use include:

  • Trauma-focused Cognitive Behavioural Therapy (TF-CBT) — addressing the thoughts, emotions, and behaviours connected to trauma
  • Narrative therapy — helping clients reframe their story and reclaim agency in how they understand their past
  • Somatic approaches — working with the body's held tension and trauma responses, not just the cognitive understanding
  • Attachment-based therapy — repairing the fundamental relational wounds that early experiences created

A Word About Healing and Forgiveness

In Uganda's faith communities, the concept of forgiveness is central — and it can sometimes be weaponised in ways that shut down healing. "You must forgive and forget" can be used to pressure survivors of childhood trauma into dismissing their pain before they have processed it. At Thrive, we understand forgiveness as a possible destination on a healing journey — not a prerequisite for beginning it. You do not have to forgive before you grieve. You do not have to protect those who hurt you in order to heal.

"The children we were had no choice about what happened to us. The adults we are have a choice about what we do with it. Healing is not about erasing the past — it is about refusing to let the past dictate the future. And that work — that brave, difficult, beautiful work — is exactly what we do together at Thrive." — Ahebwa Keisha Patience, Founder, Thrive Therapy & Wellness

If you recognise yourself in any of the signs described in this article, you are not broken — you are carrying experiences that were never properly supported. Book a consultation with our trauma-informed team at Thrive Therapy & Wellness in Ntinda, Kampala. All sessions are completely confidential, and you will never be judged for what happened to you or how it has shaped you. Your healing begins when you are ready — and we will be here.
