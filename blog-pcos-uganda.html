Hormonal Health

Understanding PCOS in Uganda: Symptoms, Causes & Natural Treatment Options

Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS) affects approximately 1 in 10 women of reproductive age worldwide — yet in Uganda it is frequently misdiagnosed, dismissed, or left entirely untreated. This article aims to change that.

Ahebwa Keisha Patience March 2025 9 min read Hormonal Health

Many Ugandan women spend years not knowing why their periods are irregular, why they are gaining weight despite eating carefully, why acne keeps returning long after adolescence, or why getting pregnant is proving so difficult. They visit clinics, take medications for individual symptoms, and receive no clear explanation. What they may have — and what may never have been named — is Polycystic Ovary Syndrome, commonly known as PCOS.

PCOS is one of the most common hormonal conditions affecting women of reproductive age globally, yet it remains widely under-recognised in Uganda. Part of this is due to limited diagnostic infrastructure. Part of it is the tendency to treat individual symptoms — such as irregular periods or weight gain — without investigating the underlying hormonal pattern. And part of it, frankly, is that women's health complaints have historically not received the attention they deserve.

At Thrive Therapy & Wellness, our hormonal health programme takes a whole-person approach to conditions like PCOS — addressing the physical, emotional, and psychological dimensions of living with a chronic hormonal condition. This article is written for every Ugandan woman who has been confused, dismissed, or simply never informed about PCOS.

What Is PCOS?

Polycystic Ovary Syndrome is a hormonal imbalance that affects the ovaries. In a typical menstrual cycle, the ovaries produce eggs that are released during ovulation. In women with PCOS, this process is disrupted — often because the body produces higher-than-normal levels of androgens (sometimes called "male hormones," although all women produce them in small amounts).

This hormonal imbalance can cause small fluid-filled sacs (follicles) to develop on the ovaries — which is where the "polycystic" part of the name comes from. These follicles prevent the ovaries from releasing eggs regularly, which leads to irregular or absent periods and, for some women, difficulty conceiving.

PCOS is a syndrome — meaning it is defined by a collection of symptoms rather than a single cause — and it presents very differently from woman to woman. Some women have all the classic symptoms; others have only one or two. This variability is part of why it is so frequently missed.

Common Symptoms of PCOS in Ugandan Women

Recognising PCOS begins with recognising its signs. In the Ugandan context, several of these symptoms are frequently normalised or attributed to other causes — which delays diagnosis and treatment.

Irregular or Absent Periods

One of the most consistent signs of PCOS is unpredictable menstruation — periods that come every 35 days or longer, that are skipped entirely for months at a time, or that come very frequently. Many Ugandan women are told that irregular periods are "just how they are" or attributed to diet, stress, or spiritual causes. While stress can certainly affect the cycle, consistently irregular periods deserve investigation.

Weight Gain and Difficulty Losing Weight

Many women with PCOS find that they gain weight easily — particularly around the abdomen — and that weight loss is extremely difficult despite healthy eating and exercise. This is not a matter of willpower or discipline. PCOS disrupts insulin and metabolism in ways that make fat storage more likely. In Uganda, where weight gain in women is sometimes culturally celebrated, PCOS-related weight gain may go unremarked or even be seen as healthy — masking the underlying hormonal issue.

Excess Hair Growth (Hirsutism)

Elevated androgens can cause hair to grow in areas where women do not typically have much — the chin, upper lip, chest, and abdomen. In Uganda, many women manage this privately through shaving or threading without ever connecting it to a hormonal condition. Hirsutism is one of the more emotionally distressing symptoms of PCOS and can significantly affect confidence and body image.

Acne and Skin Changes

Hormonal acne — particularly along the jawline, chin, and back — is a common PCOS symptom. Many women spend considerable money on topical treatments that provide temporary relief but never address the hormonal root cause. Darkening of the skin along the neck, groin, or under the arms (a condition called acanthosis nigricans) can also signal PCOS, particularly when linked to insulin resistance.

Hair Thinning or Loss

Paradoxically, while PCOS can cause excess hair in some places, it can also cause thinning of the hair on the scalp — similar to male-pattern hair loss. This symptom tends to be particularly distressing and can develop gradually over years.

Fertility Challenges

Because PCOS disrupts ovulation, it is one of the leading causes of difficulty conceiving. For Ugandan women in contexts where fertility is closely tied to social status and family expectations, an unexplained inability to conceive can cause enormous emotional distress — and frequently, PCOS is the underlying reason that has never been investigated.

What Causes PCOS?

PCOS does not have a single, simple cause. It is understood to arise from a combination of genetic, hormonal, and lifestyle factors.

Insulin Resistance

A significant proportion of women with PCOS — estimated at 65–80% — have some degree of insulin resistance, meaning the body's cells do not respond effectively to insulin. This causes the pancreas to produce more insulin to compensate. High insulin levels then signal the ovaries to produce more androgens, which disrupts the hormonal balance that governs the menstrual cycle. Insulin resistance is closely linked to diet high in refined carbohydrates, physical inactivity, and weight gain — all increasingly common in urban Uganda.

Genetics and Family History

PCOS tends to run in families. If your mother, sister, or aunt has PCOS — or has symptoms that sound similar, even if never diagnosed — your own risk is higher. The genetic link is well-established, though the precise genes involved are still being studied.

Chronic Stress and Inflammation

Chronic stress elevates cortisol levels, which in turn can worsen insulin resistance and drive up androgen production. Low-grade chronic inflammation — which is increasingly recognised as a component of PCOS — also disrupts ovarian function. In Uganda, where many women navigate multiple simultaneous stressors (economic pressure, relationship difficulties, caregiving burdens, and health concerns), the stress-PCOS connection deserves serious attention.

How PCOS Affects Mental Health

PCOS is a physical condition, but its effects on mental health are profound and often underestimated. Research consistently shows that women with PCOS have significantly higher rates of depression, anxiety, and body image distress than the general population — and this is not simply a reaction to physical symptoms. Hormonal imbalances themselves — particularly elevated androgens and disrupted oestrogen and progesterone levels — directly affect mood regulation and brain chemistry.

For Ugandan women, the mental health burden of PCOS is compounded by social factors:

  • Pressure from family to have children, combined with fertility difficulties
  • Shame or embarrassment about visible symptoms like facial hair or acne
  • Weight stigma in contexts where PCOS-related weight gain is judged morally
  • Financial stress from managing chronic health conditions with limited resources
  • Lack of accurate information, leading to confusion and fear

This is why at Thrive, we take a truly integrated approach to PCOS — addressing both the hormonal and the psychological aspects together. Many women find that counselling and emotional support are as important to their PCOS management as dietary changes or medical treatment. Our therapy services create a safe space to process the emotional dimensions of living with a chronic condition — without judgment, and with full understanding of the Ugandan cultural context.

Natural and Lifestyle Approaches to Managing PCOS

The encouraging reality about PCOS is that lifestyle changes can significantly reduce symptoms — and in some cases, restore regular ovulation without medication. This is not about quick fixes or miracle diets. It is about consistent, sustainable changes that work with the body's hormonal system.

Nutrition

Because insulin resistance underlies most cases of PCOS, a diet that stabilises blood sugar is foundational. This means eating balanced meals with protein, healthy fats, and fibre at regular intervals — rather than long periods without eating followed by large, high-carbohydrate meals. Anti-inflammatory foods rich in antioxidants also help — leafy greens, tomatoes, berries, and spices like turmeric and ginger.

Exercise and Physical Activity

Regular moderate exercise improves insulin sensitivity, supports healthy weight, reduces stress hormones, and — over time — can help restore regular ovulation. You do not need a gym. Walking briskly for 30 minutes most days of the week makes a measurable difference. Strength training is particularly effective for insulin resistance. Our physical wellness programme at Thrive supports women with PCOS in building sustainable, enjoyable movement habits.

Stress Reduction

Because chronic stress worsens PCOS through cortisol and inflammation pathways, managing stress is a genuine medical intervention — not a luxury. Consistent sleep, grounding practices, counselling, and time for rest and connection are all important parts of PCOS management.

Sleep

Poor sleep worsens insulin resistance and disrupts the hormonal signals that govern the menstrual cycle. Prioritising seven to nine hours of quality sleep each night — as difficult as this can be with work, children, and noise in urban Kampala — is one of the most important things a woman with PCOS can do for her health.

Foods That Help and Harm PCOS in the Ugandan Diet

Understanding PCOS-friendly eating does not mean abandoning Ugandan food culture — it means making informed choices within it.

Foods That Support PCOS Management

  • Beans and legumes — high in protein and fibre, they slow digestion and prevent blood sugar spikes. Kidney beans, groundnuts, and cowpeas are excellent choices.
  • Matooke — when green and less ripe, matooke has a lower glycaemic impact than when very ripe. Steamed matooke as part of a balanced meal is perfectly appropriate.
  • Leafy greens — sukuma wiki (kale), dodo (amaranth), and nakati (African nightshade) are nutrient-dense, anti-inflammatory, and widely available across Uganda.
  • Fish — tilapia and Nile perch from Lake Victoria are excellent sources of protein and omega-3 fatty acids, which reduce inflammation associated with PCOS.
  • Eggs — an affordable, nutrient-dense protein source that supports stable blood sugar.
  • Avocado — rich in healthy fats that support hormonal balance and reduce inflammation.

Foods to Limit

  • Refined posho and white bread — highly processed grains cause rapid blood sugar spikes that worsen insulin resistance. Where possible, choose whole grain options or balance with protein and vegetables.
  • Sugary drinks — sodas, sweetened juices, and packaged drinks are among the most significant drivers of insulin resistance. Water, herbal teas, and fresh juice in moderate amounts are better alternatives.
  • Deep-fried foods — frequently fried foods contribute to inflammation. Roasting, steaming, or grilling are better preparation methods.
  • Processed and packaged snacks — biscuits, crisps, and similar foods are high in refined carbohydrates and unhealthy fats that worsen insulin resistance.
  • Excessive refined sugar — including in cooking, tea, and porridge. Reducing added sugar is one of the highest-impact dietary changes for PCOS.

The goal is not perfection — it is progress. Small, consistent improvements in eating habits compound over months into significant hormonal changes. Many women find that within three to six months of dietary and lifestyle changes, their periods become more regular, their symptoms reduce, and their energy and mood improve significantly.

When to See a Specialist for PCOS

While lifestyle changes are powerful, some situations call for specialist support. You should seek professional evaluation if:

  • You have not had a period for three or more months
  • You have been trying to conceive for more than 12 months without success (or 6 months if over 35)
  • Your symptoms — weight gain, hair loss, acne, or facial hair — are significantly affecting your quality of life
  • You are experiencing depression, anxiety, or significant emotional distress related to your symptoms or fertility
  • You have a family history of diabetes or heart disease, as PCOS increases long-term metabolic risk

A diagnosis of PCOS typically involves a physical examination, blood tests to check hormone levels and blood sugar, and an ultrasound of the ovaries. In Kampala, these tests are available at multiple hospitals and private clinics, and our team at Thrive can help guide you toward appropriate medical referrals alongside holistic wellness support.
