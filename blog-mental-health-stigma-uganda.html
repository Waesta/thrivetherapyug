Mental Health

Breaking Mental Health Stigma in Uganda

Stigma remains the single greatest barrier preventing Ugandans from seeking the mental health support they need and deserve. It is time to change that conversation — for good.

Ahebwa Keisha Patience May 2025 8 min read Mental Health

Every week at Thrive Therapy & Wellness in Ntinda, Kampala, we meet people who have been suffering in silence for months — sometimes years. When we ask why they waited so long to seek help, the answer is almost always the same: "I was afraid of what people would say."

Mental health stigma in Uganda is not simply a personal problem. It is a public health crisis. It silences people at the moment they most need support, drives untreated illness deeper underground, and costs families, workplaces, and communities more than we can fully measure. But stigma is not permanent. It is learned — and what is learned can be unlearned.

What Is Mental Health Stigma?

Stigma is a set of negative beliefs, attitudes, and stereotypes that mark a person or group as different, inferior, or dangerous. When applied to mental health, stigma tells people that struggling emotionally means they are weak, cursed, dangerous, or broken. It shows up in two interconnected forms:

  • Social stigma — the judgment, discrimination, and exclusion that come from other people, families, workplaces, churches, and communities.
  • Self-stigma — internalised shame where a person begins to believe the negative things said about them. This is often the most damaging form because it stops people from even considering that help is available.

Both forms discourage help-seeking, and both are alive and active across Uganda today.

Why Mental Health Stigma Is Particularly Prevalent in Uganda

Uganda is a deeply communal society. Family reputation, respect within the community, and spiritual standing matter enormously. These values are beautiful — they create strong support networks and a deep sense of belonging. But they also create a social environment where personal struggles are expected to stay private, and where admitting to emotional difficulty can feel like a betrayal of the family's name.

Several factors make mental health stigma especially strong here:

Cultural and Community Expectations

In many Ugandan communities — whether in Buganda, Busoga, Acholi, or elsewhere — there is a cultural expectation that people, especially men, must be strong. The idea that someone might need to talk to a stranger about their feelings is seen as a sign of weakness, or even as a lack of faith. Elders are often seen as the appropriate source of wisdom, and community expectations can prevent younger generations from seeking outside help even when they desperately need it.

The Role of Religion

Uganda is one of the most religious countries in the world, and faith is a genuine source of comfort and community for millions. However, some religious interpretations frame mental illness as spiritual warfare, demonic possession, or divine punishment for sin. While prayer and faith absolutely have a place in holistic wellbeing, framing mental health conditions purely as spiritual failures can prevent people from accessing evidence-based care at the same time.

The Language Gap

Language shapes how we think about the world. In Luganda and many other Ugandan languages, there is no widely used, neutral word that normalises the concept of talking therapy the way it is understood in clinical settings. The closest expressions often carry connotations of madness ("asilamu," "mulalu") rather than everyday emotional struggle. This language gap makes it harder to have open, normalised conversations about mental health — and harder for people to see therapy as something that is for them.

Limited Awareness and Education

Mental health education is still largely absent from school curricula across Uganda. Without early, accurate information, misconceptions fill the gap — and those misconceptions tend to be rooted in fear.

The Real Cost of Untreated Mental Health in Uganda

Stigma is not harmless. When people cannot access mental health care because of shame, fear, or judgment, the consequences are severe and wide-reaching.

The World Health Organization estimates that depression and anxiety cost the global economy over USD 1 trillion each year in lost productivity. In Uganda, where large numbers of people work in demanding informal sectors, agriculture, and caregiving roles, untreated mental health conditions mean families fall apart, businesses fail, and children grow up in homes where unresolved pain is passed from one generation to the next.

Suicide rates, though under-reported due to stigma itself, are a growing public health concern in Uganda. Research consistently shows that stigma reduces the likelihood of someone reaching out before a crisis point. People who cannot talk about their pain wait until it becomes unbearable — and by then, the support they need is far more intensive.

In the workplace, unaddressed anxiety, depression, and trauma translate into absenteeism, reduced concentration, strained relationships with colleagues, and poor decision-making. Our workplace wellness programmes at Thrive exist precisely because organisations are beginning to recognise this cost.

Common Mental Health Myths in Uganda — Debunked

Let us address some of the most common beliefs about mental health that keep people from seeking help:

Myth 1: "Mental illness means you are mad (mulalu)."
Fact: The vast majority of mental health conditions — depression, anxiety, grief, trauma, relationship difficulties — have nothing to do with what most people picture when they hear the word "mad." They are common human experiences, and they are highly treatable with the right support.

Myth 2: "Therapy is for white people or the very rich."
Fact: Mental health struggles do not discriminate by race, income, or education. At Thrive, we see people from all walks of life — market vendors, university students, executives, and mothers at home. Therapy is for anyone who is struggling.

Myth 3: "If you just pray hard enough, the problem will go away."
Fact: Faith and therapy are not opposites. Many of our clients hold deep faith, and we respect that entirely. Just as prayer does not replace treatment for malaria, it is most powerful when supported by professional care for mental health conditions.

Myth 4: "Talking about problems only makes them worse."
Fact: Research consistently shows the opposite. Naming and processing our experiences — in a safe, confidential space — reduces their power over us. Suppression is what creates long-term harm.

Myth 5: "Mental illness is a punishment from God or the result of witchcraft."
Fact: Mental health conditions are rooted in biology, psychology, and life experiences. Anxiety, depression, PTSD, and other conditions have clear, well-researched causes that are treatable. Attributing them to curses or punishment causes unnecessary guilt and delays effective care.

How Communities Can Break the Stigma

Change does not only happen in therapy rooms. It happens at dinner tables, in churches, in boda-boda stages, at village meetings, and in school staffrooms. Here is how communities across Uganda can begin to shift the conversation:

  • Talk openly. When community leaders, pastors, teachers, and parents speak honestly about mental health — including their own struggles — they give others permission to do the same. Silence breeds shame; openness breeds connection.
  • Choose language carefully. Replacing words like "mulalu" with more neutral, compassionate language creates space for honest conversation. When we stop equating mental illness with danger or disgrace, more people can come forward.
  • Bring mental health into schools. Children who learn about emotions and wellbeing early are more likely to seek help as adults and less likely to stigmatise others.
  • Engage religious leaders. Some of the most powerful anti-stigma work in Uganda is happening through faith communities whose leaders have been educated about mental health. A pastor or imam who preaches compassion for mental health struggles can change thousands of minds.
  • Share stories. Testimony is powerful in Ugandan culture. When someone who has been through depression or anxiety speaks openly about their recovery, it sends a message to everyone listening: this happens to ordinary people, and it can get better.

When to Seek Professional Help

Many people are unsure when their struggles have crossed from ordinary stress into something that deserves professional attention. The truth is, you do not have to be in crisis to benefit from therapy. You are welcome to book a consultation any time you feel that your mental or emotional wellbeing is affecting your daily life.

Some signs that professional support may be helpful:

  • Persistent sadness, emptiness, or hopelessness lasting more than two weeks
  • Excessive worry that you cannot switch off, even about small things
  • Difficulty sleeping or sleeping too much
  • Withdrawing from family, friends, or activities you used to enjoy
  • Feeling easily irritated or angered in ways that feel out of character
  • Thoughts of harming yourself or others
  • Using alcohol or other substances to cope with stress or pain
  • Feeling unable to function at work, school, or as a parent

One of the most important things to know about professional therapy is that it is completely confidential. What you share in session stays in session. Your family, employer, church, or community will not know you are attending therapy unless you choose to tell them. Your privacy is protected — always.

You can be part of that change — starting today.
